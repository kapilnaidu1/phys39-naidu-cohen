Direction check, which is the point of Part 1: the thermistor is the LOWER
leg of the divider, so warming it LOWERS both the resistance and the voltage
at A0, and the reported temperature RISES. Every line above obeys this. A
reading that moved the other way would mean the two legs were swapped.

================================================================
THE FAULT WE FOUND, AND WHY IT MATTERS
================================================================

Batch 3 contains jumps that no thermal process can produce. The clearest are

    35.18 -> 33.01 -> 29.43      a 5.75 C fall across two reports
    25.57 -> 29.90 -> 26.35      a 4.3 C spike and immediate return
    27.80 -> 32.23 -> 33.12      a 4.4 C step up

Each 0.5 s report already averages 500 ADC samples, so electrical noise
cannot survive into the printed value at this amplitude. The thermistor's
own cooling time constant in air is about 15 s, so the sensor physically
cannot change by 4 C in half a second. A step that fast has to be a change
in the CIRCUIT, not in the temperature.

It was a loose thermistor lead. Reseating it dropped the scatter on a quiet
baseline by a factor of about four and removed the jumps entirely, which is
what Capture B above shows.

The lesson we are recording because it cost us the most time: before
modelling noise, check the connections. Several plausible physical
explanations were considered first, mains pickup, hand capacitance, and
convection, and each was consistent with some of the data and refuted by the
rest. None of them would ever have found a bad contact.
